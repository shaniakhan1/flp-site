BOOKING PAGE CHECKLIST

Make booking feel
like the easy part.

A useful booking page answers the questions someone has before choosing a time. This checklist helps you connect the service explanation, inquiry form, calendar, and confirmation into one understandable path.

By FLP Marketing Group · Updated September 18, 2026

IN THIS GUIDE

Explain the appointmentOne clear next stepA useful formCalendar checksConfirmationsPhone testingOngoing care

Before you share the link: check that customers know what they are booking, what it costs, what happens next, and how to reach a person. Then complete the entire process on a phone, including a real test of the calendar connection.

1. Explain the appointment before showing the calendar.

Give the service a clear name and explain who it is for. Tell people what the appointment is meant to accomplish, how long it will take, and whether it happens online, by phone, or in person. A consultation is easier to choose when the customer knows what they will leave with.

Publish the relevant price when it is fixed. If pricing depends on the project, explain how the quote is determined. State any consultation charge before booking. Avoid making someone reserve a time simply to discover a basic condition of the service.

2. Give the page one clear next step.

A service page can be short and still answer the important questions. A useful structure is:

  1. The service and the problem it helps with.
  2. Who the service is a good fit for.
  3. What is included and what the customer needs to provide.
  4. How the inquiry or booking process works.
  5. Genuine evidence, relevant background, and answers to common questions.
  6. A clearly labeled inquiry or booking action.

If the appointment needs a suitability check first, make the action “Request a consultation” and explain that the business will confirm it. Use “Book a consultation” only when the customer can actually reserve the appointment through that flow.

3. Ask for information someone will use.

Start with the details needed to respond and prepare: a name, a reliable contact method, and a short description of the request. Add location, project type, or another field only when it helps determine whether the service is a fit.

Label required fields clearly. Explain where the customer can ask a question. Keep long background questionnaires for the point where they are genuinely needed. A small inquiry form should not feel like the first hour of the project.

4. Check what the calendar actually does.

Open the link as a customer. Verify the timezone, availability, appointment duration, meeting location, and confirmation details. If your process requires staff approval, the page and emails should consistently call it a request until approval happens.

Test what happens when a customer reschedules or cancels. Decide who is notified and whether any follow-up needs to stop or change. The calendar should remain the source of truth for whether a booking exists.

5. Make the confirmation worth reading.

A form confirmation should acknowledge the request and explain the next step. A booking confirmation should show the confirmed appointment details and any preparation instructions. These are different messages, and mixing them up can leave a customer thinking they are booked when they are not.

Use the same business name and familiar tone across the page, calendar, and emails. Give people a real reply address or contact route. If reminders are part of the flow, use approved wording and a schedule that makes sense for your service.

6. Test the whole journey on a phone.

Reading the page is only the first check. Run through these steps before launch:

  • Read the opening, price, and main action at a narrow screen width without horizontal scrolling.
  • Submit the form with a missing required field, then with valid test details.
  • Check that the inquiry reaches the intended business contact.
  • Open the confirmation and use the booking link.
  • Confirm that the appointment appears in the correct calendar and timezone.
  • Reply to a test follow-up and verify that scheduled reminders stop.
  • Test the connected booking and opt-out stop signals too.

Repeat the relevant tests whenever you change the form, calendar connection, email system, or sequence. A page that looks right can still have a broken connection behind it.

7. Name the person who keeps it working.

Decide who checks for failed submissions, who updates availability and service information, and who answers replies. Give the ongoing work a defined scope so small maintenance tasks do not turn into an open-ended marketing project.
