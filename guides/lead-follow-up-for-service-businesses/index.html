INQUIRY FOLLOW-UP

Someone’s interested.
Make the next step easy.

Lead follow-up is the work between “I’m interested” and a real conversation. A useful process acknowledges the inquiry, gives the person one clear next step, and puts a human in charge when they reply.

By FLP Marketing Group · Updated September 19, 2026

IN THIS GUIDE

The useful starting point: confirm the inquiry, offer the appropriate booking link, schedule a small number of relevant reminders, and stop those reminders when someone replies, books, or opts out.

Start with the inquiry you actually receive.

Choose one service and one source of inquiries before building a sequence. A person requesting a home-organizing consultation needs a different next step from someone asking for a custom commercial proposal. Write down what they have already told you, what they still need to know, and who will answer their questions.

If your business has little or no incoming interest, fixing follow-up won’t create that demand on its own. Work out how people will find you first. If inquiries already arrive but the next step depends on remembering to send an email, this is a process worth improving.

Send a useful confirmation.

The first message should make three things clear: you received the request, the person contacted the right business, and there is an obvious next step. Use your business name and ordinary language. Include the booking link if a consultation is the appropriate next action.

ILLUSTRATIVE EMAIL

Hi Jamie, thanks for getting in touch about organizing your space. We’d love to hear what you have in mind. You can choose a consultation time using the link below. If you have a question first, just reply to this email.

A confirmation is an acknowledgment. It should not promise a quote, appointment, or delivery date your business has not agreed to. If you publish a response time, make it one your team can consistently meet.

Plan the reminders before you need them.

There isn’t one follow-up schedule that works for every service. Choose timing based on how quickly customers normally make a decision and how much information they need. A short consultation and a large custom project can require very different approaches.

As an initial workflow to review, send the confirmation when the form is submitted, a brief check-in after two business days, and a final invitation several days later. This is an illustrative starting point, not a proven conversion formula. Adjust it using your own replies, bookings, and customer feedback.

Give each message a purpose. The first can make booking easier. A later message can answer a common question. The last can leave the door open without adding pressure. More emails are useful only when they make the decision easier.

Make the stop rules part of the build.

A customer who has already replied should not receive “just checking in” reminders from the same sequence. Test the signals that stop it:

  • A reply: stop the automated reminders and route the conversation to the named business contact.
  • A booking: use the connected calendar’s confirmed event as the signal. A click on a booking link is not proof that an appointment was made.
  • An opt-out: stop the sequence and respect the person’s preference.

If a tool cannot reliably provide one of these signals, resolve that gap before launch. Be clear about any manual step and who owns it.

Decide where a person takes over.

Approved emails can repeat information your business has already agreed to share. Personal questions, unusual requests, pricing exceptions, and sales conversations need a named owner. Make sure that person knows where replies arrive and how to see the context.

Automation should make this handoff easier to notice. It should not invent an answer about availability, service suitability, or price. The business needs to assign a person to handle these conversations.

Measure what happened, then improve one thing.

Keep a simple record of inquiries, messages delivered, replies, bookings, and exceptions that needed attention. Review results over a consistent period. A booking after a follow-up is useful activity to report, but it does not prove the email caused additional revenue.

Use what you learn to make a focused change: clarify the booking step, answer a recurring question, or adjust the reminder timing. Check the flow again after changes to the form, email settings, or calendar.

When is a managed service useful?

Doing it yourself can make sense when the workflow is simple and someone has time to build, test, and maintain it. A managed service can be useful when that work keeps getting postponed and the value of handling existing inquiries justifies the full ongoing cost.
